**The housing reform (CU31)** is the day's anchor event. Sweden's rental housing market — characterised by decades of utility-value (bruksvärde) rent regulation, long queues for rent-controlled apartments (bostadsrättsköer), and a dysfunctional secondary market — is being structurally reformed. The government's CU31 package introduces graduated market rents for new-build properties, modifies the bruksvärde system, and expands landlord flexibility. This aligns with the Tidö agreement's commitment to market liberalisation, is celebrated by property industry (Fastighetsägarna, HSB) and opposed by tenant movements (Hyresgästföreningen) and the left-centre opposition (S, V, MP, C).

These are technical-administrative measures with medium political salience but high implementation consequence.

## Pass 2 — Analytical Refinements

### The Dual-Track Housing Market (CU31 Technical Detail)

The CU31 reform creates Sweden's first **dual-track rental housing market** since the 1942 wartime rent freeze that established the bruksvärde system. The political economy is complex:

**Track A — Existing rent-controlled stock**: Bruksvärde negotiation between Hyresgästföreningen and Fastighetsägarna continues. Rents rise by negotiated annual increments (typically 2-4%). ~1.8M units affected.

**Track B — New-build market-rent units**: Landlord sets market rent. Initial lettings in Stockholm CBD could be 80-120% above Track A equivalent. ~50,000 new units/year at current construction rates.

The political controversy centres on the medium-term dynamic: as Track B units enter the market and landlords observe the rent premium, they have financial incentive to convert Track A units to Track B by: (a) selling to co-operative (bostadsrätt conversion — legal but limited by tenant purchase rights), (b) demolishing and rebuilding as Track B (requires demolition permit — currently strict in many municipalities). Hyresgästföreningen's legal strategy will focus on preventing this conversion pathway.
